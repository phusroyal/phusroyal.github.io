## About BlueDot's Puzzle #1 {#the-puzzle}

<a href="https://bluedot.org/puzzles/technical-ai-safety">BlueDot's Technical AI Safety Puzzle #1</a> provides a trained 5-layer MLP that performs multi-class classification over eight binary features using mean-pooled representations from a sentence transformer. The puzzle indicates nonlinear behavior at the output of the third ReLU, denoted as $\ell$, and asks participants to:

- Find the nonlinear feature, denoted as $f$.
- Explain the geometry used by the model at $\ell$ to represent $f$.
- Train a new model with a more interpretable representation.

This article addresses the first two tasks: identifying the nonlinear feature and explaining its basin-like representation. The companion article trains a new model with a chosen manifold representation.

## 1. Finding the Nonlinear Feature with a Linear Probe {#finding-the-nonlinear-feature}

### 1.1 Why Linear Probe (LP)? {#why-linear-probe}

I begin with the puzzle’s direct hint that feature $f$ is nonlinear. Given that clue, the most plausible first step is to train a linear probe (LP) <a class="article-citation" href="#ref-1">[1]</a>, <a class="article-citation" href="#ref-2">[2]</a>.

The idea is simple: given a set of features (which I may sometimes refer to as labels) and their corresponding activations, can we find a linear separator that predicts those features from the activations extracted at layer $\ell$?

If a feature can be accurately predicted by a linear probe, then its representation at $\ell$ is **likely linearly separable**. Conversely, if one feature consistently resists linear classification while the others are easily separated, that is a strong signal that the model uses a nonlinear representation for that feature.

### 1.2 Experiment Settings and Metrics {#experiment-settings-and-metrics}

For each feature, I train an independent linear probe on the activations at layer $\ell$ to predict the corresponding binary label. Because the features are independent, each probe is a logistic-regression classifier with an L2 penalty that limits unnecessary capacity.

Using the provided test set, I evaluate the trained probes with two main questions:

1. *How well does the probe classify the feature?*
   - **LP AUC**: measures how well the probe ranks positive examples above negative examples across all possible thresholds.
   - **LP accuracy**: the fraction of correct predictions.
2. *How cleanly are the two classes separated along the learned linear direction?*
   - **Margin gap**: the distance between the average positive-class and negative-class decision scores. A large gap means the two classes are far apart along the learned linear direction, suggesting that the feature is represented linearly.
   - **Overlap**: the fraction of examples that fall on the wrong side of the probe’s decision boundary. Lower overlap indicates cleaner linear separation between the two classes.

From <a class="article-reference" href="#table-1">Table 1</a>, seven features are recovered almost perfectly by their corresponding linear probes. Their LP AUC values are all at least 0.995, and their decision margins show strong separation between positive and negative examples.

The **country** feature, however, is different. Its LP AUC is about 0.473 and its LP accuracy is about 0.489, both near chance. Its margin gap is nearly zero, and more than half of the test examples fall on the wrong side of the probe’s decision boundary. This is not a small degradation relative to the other features; it is a sharp failure of linear separation.

At this point, it would be tempting to conclude that **country** is the nonlinearly represented feature at layer $\ell$. Before making that claim, however, I perform several sanity checks.

### 1.4 Sanity Check 1: Tail Sanity Check {#tail-sanity-check}

Linear probes cannot distinguish between two possibilities:

- The feature is nonlinear.
- The feature is not present.

Using the extracted activations, this sanity check verifies that the model’s original tail can still predict **country** correctly from the layer $\ell$ activations. If the model’s tail achieves high AUC and accuracy, then the information is present at $\ell$ and decoded nonlinearly downstream.

This check also serves as a control against silly mistakes, such as:

- Wrong layer extraction.
- Wrong feature ordering.
- Shuffling activations against labels.
- Batching mismatches.

The tail result is high, country reaches an AUC of 0.9938 and an accuracy of 0.9640 on 1,500 test examples. This means that the country signal remains usable by the original model tail from the extracted activations, even though a separately trained linear probe fails to recover it.

### 1.5 Sanity Check 2: Causal Steering {#causal-steering}

**Motivation.** I test the current findings from a different angle. If a feature is linearly represented, it should also be associated with a meaningful direction. Moving activations along that direction should systematically increase or decrease the corresponding feature logit <a class="article-citation" href="#ref-3">[3]</a>. To test this idea, I use causal steering.

**Causal Steering.** For each feature, I define a candidate direction:

$$
d_f = \mu_{f,1} - \mu_{f,0},
$$

where $\mu_{f,1}$ is the mean layer $\ell$ activation for positive examples of feature $f$, and $\mu_{f,0}$ is the mean for negative examples.

I then intervene on the layer $\ell$ activations as:

$$
h' = h + \alpha \hat d_k, \quad \hat d_k = \frac{d_k}{\lVert d_k \rVert},
$$

where $\alpha \in [-5,5]$ is the steering strength, before passing the activations through the remaining model tail.

**Intuition.** For a linear feature, increasing $\alpha \in [-5,5]$ should produce a consistent change in the feature logit. For a nonlinear feature, steering should be weaker or less consistent, suggesting that the feature is not controlled by a single global direction but instead depends on a more structured or multi-region representation.

**Metrics.** Let $C_{f}(\cdot)$ be the model’s logit for feature $f$,

$$
\bar C_f^{(k)}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{N_{\text{test}}}\sum_{i}C_f(h_i+\alpha \hat d_k)
$$

Here $k$ is the feature whose direction we steer along, and $f$ is the output logit being measured. As we increase $\alpha \in [-5,5]$ in steps of $t$:

- **Monotonicity:** measures whether the target score moves in the intended direction as steering strength increases; for example, the fraction of adjacent steps where

$$
\bar C_f^{(k)}(\alpha_t) -\bar C_f^{(k)}(\alpha_{t-1}) > 0.
$$

- **Target logit delta:** checks whether steering along the direction changes the intended feature by a meaningful amount. Computed as the signed target logit change between $\alpha=-5$ and $\alpha=5$:

$$
\bar C_f^{(k)}(5) - \bar C_f^{(k)}(-5).
$$

- **Max off-target delta:** computes the largest unintended absolute change among all other features:

$$
\max_{f \neq \text{k}} |\bar C_f^{(k)}(5) - \bar C_f^{(k)}(-5)|
$$

- **Specificity ratio:** computes how focused the steering is by taking the ratio of the *target logit delta* to the *maximum off-target delta*. It summarizes whether the target movement is large relative to the largest off-target side effect.

**Results.** From <a class="article-reference" href="#table-2">Table 2</a>, the non-country features show the expected behavior of a simple direction. Their target-logit curves are perfectly monotonic over the alpha sweep, and their target movement is far larger than the largest off-target movement. ### 1.6 Question 1 Conclusion {#question-1-conclusion}

The feature most likely to be represented nonlinearly is **country**.

At layer $\ell$, most features appear as linearly accessible directions: a linear probe recovers them, and adding their train-set mean-difference directions causally moves the matching downstream logit. Label country breaks both patterns. Its linear probe is near chance, its class-mean direction is almost zero, and steering along that direction mainly changes other logits instead of country. Yet the frozen downstream tail still predicts the country label accurately, so the information is still present. 

## 2. Explaining the Country Basin {#explaining-the-country-basin}

### 2.1 Short Answer {#short-answer}
*Country is not represented as a linear region. It is closer to an **inside/outside region, or a basin-like region**, in the layer $\ell$ activation space.*

In the plots and analysis below, country-positive activations sit near a compact central region, while many country-negative activations lie farther out around it. The downstream tail can decode this because the next ReLU layer can combine several local halfspaces into a curved boundary <a class="article-citation" href="#ref-4">[4]</a>.

### 2.2 Why Local Gradients? {#why-local-gradients}

After Question 1, two findings remain:

- A linear probe cannot recover country from layer $\ell$.
- The original model tail can still recover country from the same activations.

My next hypothesis is that the next ReLU layer might decode country using different local directions in different regions of activation space.

The idea is simple. The tail contains ReLUs, so it behaves like different linear maps in different parts of hidden space. Near one hidden state $h$, the ReLU on/off pattern is fixed, so the feature logit has one local direction. Near another hidden state, the on/off pattern can be different, so the local direction can point somewhere else. This is the piecewise-linear behavior of ReLU networks  <a class="article-citation" href="#ref-4">[4]</a>.

For a feature $f$ logit, the remaining tail is roughly:

$$
z_f(h)=w_f^\top \operatorname{ReLU}(Ah+b)+c_f
$$

Then the local gradient is:

$$
g_f(h)=\nabla_h z_f(h)=w_f^\top \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{1}_{Ah+b>0})A
$$

This gradient is a vector showing the precise direction to nudge a specific activation $h$ to rapidly increase the feature score, calculated by mapping the input change through only the current active pathways ($\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{1}_{Ah+b>0})$) of the network. This is similar in spirit to using class-score gradients to inspect what input directions support a class  <a class="article-citation" href="#ref-5">[5]</a>.

My expectation is:

- For a linear feature, these local gradients should point in almost the same direction across examples.
- For a nonlinear feature, these local gradients should rotate, split, or spread across examples.

### 2.3 Experiment Settings and Metrics {#basin-experiment-settings-and-metrics}

For all experiments in this section, I use the extracted test activations at hidden-2 post-ReLU. I use three measurements:

1. **Local gradient cosine:** normalize every local gradient and measure mean pairwise cosine within each feature. This checks whether one local direction is reused across examples.
2. **Gradient PCA:** stack all normalized local gradients for all eight features, fit one shared 2D PCA, and summarize each feature’s point cloud by centroid and radius.
3. **Representation geometry:** project the actual layer $\ell$ activations into a 3D basis.

### 2.4 Local Gradient Consistency {#local-gradient-consistency}

For each feature, I normalize every local gradient:

$$
\hat g_f(h_i)=\frac{g_f(h_i)}{\lVert g_f(h_i) \rVert+\epsilon}
$$

Then I compute the mean pairwise cosine between local gradients:

$$
\kappa_f=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i\ne j}\hat g_f(h_i)^\top \hat g_f(h_j)
$$

If $\kappa_f$ is:
- **close to 1:** the feature has almost the same local direction everywhere.<br><span class="interpretation-note">→ This is expected behavior for linear features, where the model is using one global direction.</span>

- **much lower:** the decoder is changing direction across examples.<br><span class="interpretation-note">→ This is expected for country, where the model is using different local directions in different ReLU regions.</span>

### 2.5 Gradient PCA {#gradient-pca}

To inspect the gradients, I stack the normalized local gradients for all features and fit one shared PCA over them. The method is:

$$
G_{\mathrm{all}}=\{\hat g_f(h_i): f\in \text{features}, i\in \text{test examples}\}
$$

Fit one PCA on $G_{\mathrm{all}}$

$$
Z=\operatorname{PCA}_2(G_{\mathrm{all}})
$$

then plot each feature’s gradients in the same 2D PCA space.

**Expected results:**

- Linear features should form tight clusters because their local directions are almost constant.
- **Country** should be spread out or split into multiple clusters because it has several directions.

For each feature cloud, I then compute its centroid and the radius of each point:

$$
\bar z_f=\frac{1}{N_f}\sum_{i:y_i=f} z_{i},  \quad r_i=\lVert z_i-\bar z_f \rVert_2
$$

I then summarize each feature by its mean radius and 95% radius.

### 2.6 Looking at the Activations {#looking-at-the-activations}

Gradient PCA shows how the decoder changes, but I also need the representation itself. I therefore examine the actual layer $\ell$ activations.

For each feature $f$, I project its activations into a **feature-specific 3D subspace fitted from that feature’s normalized local gradients**. I choose this projection to capture the directions in which the feature decoder changes, rather than only the directions in which the raw activations have the most variance. The following figures therefore ask: what shape do the layer $\ell$ activations take in directions selected by the local decoder geometry for feature $f$?

For a feature $f$, I take its usable normalized local gradients $G_f=\{\hat g_f(h_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$. Then I fit a 3D PCA basis on those gradients

$$
U_f=\operatorname{PCA}_3(G_f)
$$

and project the test activations into the corresponding feature-specific basis after removing the mean:

$$
q_{i,f}=U_f(h_i-\bar h_f)
$$

### 2.7 Radius and Mahalanobis Sanity Check {#radius-and-mahalanobis-sanity-check}

***If country is an inside / basin-like region, can distance from the center recover it?***

Here, I again use training data to fit the geometry and test data to score it. The 3D gradient basis is fitted from training hidden-2-post-ReLU activations and local gradients, then applied to the test activations.

$$
U_c=\operatorname{PCA}_3(\{\hat g_c(h_i)\}_{i\in\mathrm{train}}), \quad z_i=U_c^\top(h_i-\bar h_{\mathrm{train}})
$$

I convert “inside the basin” into distance scores:
1. **Euclidean radius:** this is the simplest score, using the projected origin $r_i=\lVert z_i \rVert_2$. If country behaves as a basin-like region with positive samples near the center, then a smaller radius should indicate country.

$$
s^{\mathrm{radius}}_i=-r_i
$$

2. **Mahalanobis radius:** this is a complementary check that allows the inside region to be elliptical rather than circular. I fit the positive samples’ mean and covariance in the first two projected dimensions.

$$
\mu_+=\mathbb{E}[z_{1:2}\mid y_c=1], \quad \Sigma_+=\operatorname{Cov}(z_{1:2}\mid y_c=1)+\lambda I
$$

Then the Mahalanobis radius measures how far a point is from the positive center while accounting for the positive class’s shape, so distance along a naturally wide direction counts less than distance along a tight direction:

$$
m_i=\sqrt{(z_{i,1:2}-\mu_+)^\top\Sigma_+^{-1}(z_{i,1:2}-\mu_+)}
$$

The score is:

$$
s^{\mathrm{maha}}_i=-m_i
$$

Then I test:
- **Linear probe AUC:** a linear probe on the same 3D coordinates.
- **Euclidean radius AUC:** scores examples by negative distance from the projected center.
- **Mahalanobis radius AUC:** scores examples by negative Mahalanobis distance using regularized positive-class covariance.
- **Quadratic probe AUC:** logistic regression on linear, squared, and pairwise product terms of the 3D coordinates.
- **Frozen tail logit AUC:** reuses the result from <a class="article-section-reference" href="#tail-sanity-check">Section 1.4</a> for reference.

For the quadratic probe I used:

$$
\phi(z)=[z_1,z_2,z_3,z_1^2,z_2^2,z_3^2,z_1z_2,z_1z_3,z_2z_3]
$$

### 2.8 Score Landscape {#score-landscape}

Before conducting the analysis above, I asked a different question:
**What does the frozen tail’s country score look like on a simple activation plane?**

Given a nonlinear feature, what does the frozen tail’s country score look like as I move through a simple 2D slice of layer $\ell$ activation space? Later, I recognized this as another sanity check: if country were linear, the tail should resemble a single tilted plane everywhere. This slice misses the full activation geometry and does not prove how country is represented in the complete 64D space.

I first chose a shared activation plane:

$$
h(x,y)=h_0+xu_1+yu_2
$$

where $u_1$ and $u_2$ are the first two activation-PCA directions. I then evaluate the frozen tail’s country logit:

$$
C_c(x,y)=C_c(h(x,y))
$$

The zero contour is

$$
\Gamma_c=\{(x,y): C_c(x,y)=0\}
$$
