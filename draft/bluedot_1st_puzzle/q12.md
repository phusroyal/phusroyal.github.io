# About the Puzzle

The puzzle provides a trained 5-layer MLP on mean-pooled representations of texts from a sentence transformer to perform a multi-class classification task over 8 different binary features. The puzzle hints that there is a non-linear behavior at the output of the third ReLU, denoted as $\ell$, and asks participants to:
- Find the non-linear feature, denoted as $f$.
- Explain the geometry used by the model at $\ell$ to represent $f$.
- Train a new model with a more interesting representation.

# 1. Find the Non-linear Feature with Linear Probe

## 1.1 Why Linear Probe (LP)?

I first use the direct hint from the puzzle that the feature $f$ is non-linear. Given that clue, the most plausible first move is to train a Linear Probe (LP) [1,2].

The idea is simple: given a set of features (which I may sometimes refer to as labels) and their corresponding activations, can we find a linear separator that predicts those features from the activations extracted at layer $\ell$?

If a feature can be accurately predicted by a linear probe, then its representation at $\ell$ is likely linearly separable. On the other hand, if one feature consistently resists linear classification while the others are easily separated, that would be a strong signal that the model is using a non-linear representation for that feature.

## 1.2 Experiment Settings and Metrics

For each feature, I train an independent linear probe using the activations at layer $\ell$ to predict the corresponding binary label as these features are independent. The probe is a logistic regression classifier with an L2 penalty to avoid giving it unnecessary capacity (or, in other words, restricting it from learning overly complicated rules).

Using the provided test set, I evaluate the trained probes with two main questions:

1. How well does the probe classify the feature?
   - AUC: measures how well the probe ranks positive examples above negative examples across all possible thresholds.
   - Accuracy: the fraction of correct predictions.
2. How cleanly are the two classes separated along the learned linear direction?
   - Margin gap: the distance between the average positive-class and negative-class decision scores. A large gap means the two classes are far apart along the learned linear direction, suggesting that the feature is represented linearly.
   - Overlap: The fraction of examples that fall on the wrong side of the probe’s decision boundary. Lower overlap indicates cleaner linear separation between the two classes.

From Table 1, seven features are recovered almost perfectly by their corresponding linear probes. Their AUC values are all at least around 0.995, and their decision margins show strong separation between positive and negative examples.

The feature country, however, is really different. Its AUC is about 0.473 and its accuracy is about 0.489, both near chance. Its margin gap is nearly zero, and more than half of the test examples fall on the wrong side of the probe’s decision boundary. This is not a small degradation relative to the other features; it is a sharp failure of linear separation.

At this point, it would be tempting to conclude that country is the non-linear feature represented at layer $\ell$. However, before making that claim, I want to perform a few sanity checks.

## 1.4 Sanity Check 1: Tail Sanity Check

Linear probes cannot distinguish between two possibilities:

- The feature is non-linear.
- The feature is not present.

Using the extracted activations, this sanity check verifies that the model’s original tail can still predict country correctly from the layer $\ell$ activations. If the model’s tail performs well with high AUC and accuracy, then the information is present at $\ell$ and is decoded non-linearly downstream.

This check also serves as a control against silly mistakes, such as:

- Wrong layer extraction.
- Wrong feature ordering.
- Shuffling activations against labels.
- Batching mismatches.

The tail result is high, country reaches an AUC of 0.9938 and an accuracy of 0.9640 on 1500 test examples. This means that country signal is still usable by the original model tail from the extracted activation, even though a separately trained linear probe fails to recover it.

## 1.5 Sanity Check 2: Causal Steering

**Motivation.** I then want to challenge the current findings from a different angle. If a feature is linearly represented, it should also be represented by a meaningful direction. Moving activations along that direction should systematically increase or decrease the corresponding feature logit [3]. To test this idea, I use causal steering.

**Causal Steering.** For each feature, define a candidate direction:

$$
d_f = \mu_{f,1} - \mu_{f,0},
$$

where $\mu_{f,1}$ is the mean layer $\ell$ activation for positive examples of feature $f$, and $\mu_{f,0}$ is the mean for negative examples.

I then intervene on the layer $\ell$ activations as
$$
h' = h + \alpha \hat d_k, \quad \hat d_k = \frac{d_k}{\|d_k\|}
$$
, where $\alpha \in [5,5]$ is steering strength, before passing them through the remaining model tail.

**Intuition.** For a linear feature, increasing $\alpha \in [-5,5]$ should produce a consistent change in the feature logit. For a non-linear feature, steering should be weaker or less consistent, suggesting that the feature is not controlled by a single global direction but instead depends on a more structured or multi-region representation.

**Metrics.** Let $C_{f}(\cdot)$ be the model's logit for feature $f$ .
$$\bar C_f^{(k)}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{N_{\text{test}}}\sum_{i}C_f(h_i+\alpha \hat d_k)$$
Here $k$ is the feature whose direction we steer along, and $f$ is the output logit being measured. As we increase $\alpha \in [-5,5]$ in steps of $t$:

- **Monotonicity:** computes whether the target score moves in the intended direction as steering strength increases, e.g., the fraction of adjacent steps where
$$
\bar C_f^{(k)}(\alpha_t) -\bar C_f^{(k)}(\alpha_{t-1}) > 0.
$$

- **Target logit delta:** checks whether steering along the direction changes the intended feature by a meaningful amount. Computed as the signed target logit change between $\alpha=-5$ and $\alpha=5$:

$$
\bar C_f^{(k)}(5) - \bar C_f^{(k)}(-5).
$$

- **Max off-target delta:** computes the largest unintended absolute change among all other features:

$$
\max_{f \neq \text{k}} |\bar C_f^{(k)}(5) - \bar C_f^{(k)}(-5)|
$$

- **Specificity ratio:** Computes how focused the steering is by taking the ratio of the target logit delta to the maximum off-target delta. It summarizes whether the target movement is large relative to the biggest off-target side effect.

**Results.** From Table 2., the non-country features show the expected behavior of a simple direction. Their target-logit curves are perfectly monotonic over the alpha sweep, and their target movement is far larger than the largest off-target movement. For example, label food has a target delta of 269.5 and max off-target delta of 14.4, giving a specificity ratio of 18.6. Label person has a target delta of 131.5 and max off-target delta of only 8.3.

Label country is again different:
- Its target-logit monotonicity is 0.51, Figure 1 shows an inconsistent curve rather than a clean directional effect.
- Its endpoint target-logit delta is only 14.9, much smaller than every other feature.
- Its largest off-target endpoint movement is 197.2, so the normalized country direction moves another logit far more than it moves the country logit.
- Its specificity ratio is 0.07, while all other features are above 6.5.

## 1.6 Question 1 Conclusion

The likely non-linearly represented feature is country.

At layer $\ell$, most features appear as linearly accessible directions: a linear probe recovers them, and adding their train-set mean-difference directions causally moves the matching downstream logit. Label country breaks both patterns. Its linear probe is near chance, its class-mean direction is almost zero, and steering along that direction mainly changes other logits instead of country. Yet the frozen downstream tail still predicts the country label accurately, so the information is still present.

The most defensible interpretation is that the country label is encoded in a nonlinear, multidimensional, or entangled pattern that the original tail can use but a single linear probe or single global direction cannot isolate.

# 2. Explain How Country Is Represented

## 2.1 Short Answer
*Country is not represented as a linear region. It is closer to an **inside/outside region, or a basin-like region**, in the layer $\ell$ activation space.*

In the plots and analysis below, country-positive activations sit near a compact central region, while many country-negative activations sit farther out around it. The downstream tail can decode this because the next ReLU layer can combine several local halfspaces into a curved boundary [4].

## 2.2 Why Local Gradients?

After Question 1, there are two things:

- A linear probe cannot recover country from layer $\ell$.
- The original model tail can still recover country from the same activations.

My next guess is that the next ReLU layer might decode country using different local directions in different regions of activation space.

The idea is simple. Around one activation $h$, the ReLU gates in the tail are fixed, so the tail is locally linear. Around another activation, a different set of gates may be active, so the local direction can change. This is the piecewise-linear behavior of ReLU networks [4].

For a feature $f$ logit, the remaining tail roughly as:

$$
z_f(h)=w_f^\top \operatorname{ReLU}(Ah+b)+c_f
$$

Then the local gradient is:

$$
g_f(h)=\nabla_h z_f(h)=w_f^\top \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{1}_{Ah+b>0})A
$$

This gradient is a vector showing the precise direction to nudge a specific activation $h$ ($A$) to rapidly increase the feature score, calculated by mapping the input change through only the current active pathways ($\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{1}_{Ah+b>0})$) of the network. This is similar in spirit to using class-score gradients to inspect what input directions support a class [5].

My expectation is:

- For a linear feature, these local gradients should point in almost the same direction across examples.
- For a non-linear feature, these local gradients should rotate, split, or spread across examples.

## 2.3 Experiment Settings and Metrics

For all experiments in this section, I use the extracted test activation at hidden 2 post-ReLU. I used three measurements:

1. **Local gradient cosine:** normalize every local gradient and measure mean pairwise cosine within each feature. This checks whether one local direction is reused across examples.
2. **Gradient PCA:** stack all normalized local gradients for all eight features, fit one shared 2D PCA, and summarize each feature’s point cloud by centroid and radius.
3. **Representation geometry:** project the actual layer $\ell$ activations into a 3D basis.

## 2.4 Local Gradient Consistency

For each feature, I normalize every local gradient:

$$
\hat g_f(h_i)=\frac{g_f(h_i)}{\|g_f(h_i)\|+\epsilon}
$$

Then I compute the mean pairwise cosine between local gradients:

$$
\kappa_f=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i\ne j}\hat g_f(h_i)^\top \hat g_f(h_j)
$$

If $\kappa_f$ is:
- **close to 1:** the feature has almost the same local direction everywhere.
  → This is expected behavior for linear features, where the model is using one global direction.

- **much lower:** the decoder is changing direction across examples.
  → This is expected for country, where the model is using different local directions in different ReLU regions.

Most features are very stable. They have local-gradient cosines around 0.9600 to 0.9862. Even sentiment, which is less tight, is still 0.8898. Country is different again. Its score is only 0.5442. The model is using different country directions in different local regions.

## 2.5 Gradient PCA

Now I want to see the gradients so I stack the normalized local gradients for all features and fit one shared PCA over them. The method is

$$
G_{\mathrm{all}}=\{\hat g_f(h_i): f\in \text{features}, i\in \text{test examples}\}
$$

Fit one PCA on $G_{\mathrm{all}}$

$$
Z=\operatorname{PCA}_2(G_{\mathrm{all}})
$$

then plot each feature’s gradients in the same 2D PCA space.

**Expected results:**

- Linear features should form tight clusters because their local directions are almost constant.
- **Country** should be spread out or split into multiple clusters because it has several directions

For each feature cloud, I then compute their centroids and radius of each point:

$$
\bar z_f=\frac{1}{N_f}\sum_{i:y_i=f} z_{i},  \quad r_i=\|z_i-\bar z_f\|_2
$$

Then I summarize each features by its mean radius, and 95% radius

The normal features form tight clouds. Their local arrows all point to roughly the same place. Country spreads into a broad curved band. These results are reported using 12,000 normalized gradient rows (8 features x 1,500 test samples).

## 2.6 Looking at the Activations

Gradient PCA shows how the decoder changes, but I need the representation itself. Let’s look at the actual layer $\ell$ activations.

Then, for a feature $f$, I project its activations into a feature-specific 3D/2D subspace fitted from that feature’s own normalized local gradient. I choose this projection because I want the directions where the feature decoder changes, not just the directions where the raw activations have the most variance. So that the following figures will tell: what shape do the layer $\ell$ activations have in directions selected by the local decoder geometry for feature $f$?

For a feature $f$, I take its usable normalized local gradients $G_f=\{\hat g_f(h_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$. Then I fit a 3D PCA basis on those gradients
$$
U_f=\operatorname{PCA}_3(G_f)
$$
and project the test activation into corresponding feature-specific basis before removing the mean:
$$
q_{i,f}=U_f(h_i-\bar h_f)
$$

Note: the 3D PCA visualizations are shown as GIFs; for the best viewing experience, open this note in a web browser.

This is the first plot where the representation feels visible. I expect maybe a messy split into several clusters. Instead, the country-positive examples look concentrated near a central region, while many negative examples sit around the outside.
→ So I start thinking of the representation of country inside a basin-like region.

One caveat is that these visualizations are not showing their clean manifolds. The full representation is still 64-dimensional, and this is only a 3D decoder-relevant view.

## 2.7 Radius and Mahalanobis Sanity Check

*If country is an inside / basin-like region, can distance from the center recover it?*

Here again I use train data to fit the geometry and test data to score it. The 3D gradient basis is fitted from train hidden-2-post-ReLu activations and local gradients, then applied to the test activations.
$$
U_c=\operatorname{PCA}_3(\{\hat g_c(h_i)\}_{i\in\mathrm{train}}), \quad z_i=U_c^\top(h_i-\bar h_{\mathrm{train}})
$$

I converted “inside the basin” into distance scores:
1. **Euclidean radius:** this is the simplest score using the projected origin $r_i=\|z_i\|_2$. If the country behaves basin-like where the positive samples near the center, then the smaller radius should mean more country.
$$
s^{\mathrm{radius}}_i=-r_i
$$

2. **Mahalanobis radius:** this is a more complicated or can be a different check for the inside region to be elliptical instead of circular. I fit the positive samples mean and covariance in the first two projected dimensions.

$$
\mu_+=\mathbb{E}[z_{1:2}\mid y_c=1], \quad \Sigma_+=\operatorname{Cov}(z_{1:2}\mid y_c=1)+\lambda I
$$

	Then the Mahalanobis radius measures how far a point is from the positive center, but stretches/shrinks the distance by the positive class’s own shape, so distance along a naturally wide direction counts less than distance along a tight direction:
$$
m_i=\sqrt{(z_{i,1:2}-\mu_+)^\top\Sigma_+^{-1}(z_{i,1:2}-\mu_+)}
$$
	And the score is:
$$
s^{\mathrm{maha}}_i=-m_i
$$
Then I test:
- **Linear probe AUC:** a linear probe on the same 3D coordinates.
- **Euclidean radius AUC:** scores examples by negative distance from the projected center.
- **Mahalanobis radius AUC:** scores examples by negative Mahalanobis distance using regularized positive-class covariance.
- **Quadratic probe AUC:** logistic regression on linear, squared, and pairwise product terms of the 3D coordinates.
- **Frozen tail logit AUC:** reuse the Section 1.4 here for reference.

For the quadratic probe I used:

$$
\phi(z)=[z_1,z_2,z_3,z_1^2,z_2^2,z_3^2,z_1z_2,z_1z_3,z_2z_3]
$$

> Figure 4. Left: country radial-basin view. Right: food radial-basin view as control. In the country’s tail-gradient projection, positive examples concentrate near the center while negatives lie farther out.

From both Table 5 and Figure 4, country-positive examples concentrate near the center in the country’s tail-gradient projection. Radius and Mahalanobis distance recover country almost as well as the frozen tail while the linear separator in this analysis is chance. The same radial-basin test does not separate food. This suggests the radius effect is specific to country, not a generic artifact of the projection.

# 3. Train a New Model with Predefined Manifold Representation

For question 3, I want to make the representation interesting in a controlled way.

*Can we choose a representation geometry first, then train the model so that
the feature follows that geometry while still solving the original task?*

This is inspired by recent work showing that neural representations can contain meaningful curved geometries, not only linear directions. Gurnee et al. [6] study counting manifolds in language models, where internal computation manipulates structured manifolds. Wurgaft et al. [7] study manifold steering, where steering along a learned curved representation can be more faithful than steering along a straight line. I use the same broad intuition, but in the opposite direction: instead of discovering a manifold after training, I build a small manifold code first and ask the model to use it.

The important difference is that I do not want the manifold to be decorative. The target feature should actually be decoded from that geometry. So the experiment needs to test not only

*Did the hidden activations form the desired shape?*

but also:

*Did the model actually use that shape to solve the task?*

In this section, I will show how gradually I achieved this. I train a new but the same 5 layer MLP with ReLUs. The target feature, country, again is encoded through a reserved 3D geometry code at hidden layer 2. I am able to guide the model to use either a sphere shell or helix tube representation within a controlled 3D subspace while preserving task performance and passing causal-use checks.

The word “use” is the key. A manifold can be visible and still be useless. A model could place points in a desired subspace while solving the task from a hidden linear shortcut somewhere else. So here is my design:

1. The geometry is specified before training.
2. The learned geometry code matches the specified geometry.
3. The original eight-label task still works.
4. Easy shortcuts, especially linear access and complement-only access, are weak.
5. Intervening on the geometry code changes the country prediction.

The rest of this answer describes the stage-by-stage process used to establish these checks.

## 3.1 Define the Target Geometry Before Training

The first stage has no model. I only define what the model will later be asked to learn.

### The Distributional Problem

Let $i$ index an example in the dataset. Let $f^\star=\text{country}$ mean the target feature I am trying to put on a manifold. The dataset gives $y_i^{f^\star}$, the binary label for the target feature on example $i$. If $y_i^{f^\star}=1$, the target feature is present, and vice versa.

That binary label does not give a coordinate such as a helix angle or sphere direction. For example, if the geometry is a helix, the label may say:

$$
y_i^{f^\star}=1.
$$

It does not say:

$$
t_i=1.7,\quad \rho_i=0.15,\quad \varphi_i=3.2.
$$

Here, $t_i$ would be a helix position, $\rho_i$ would be a radius, and $\varphi_i$ would be a tube angle for example $i$. These values are not observed.

Let $z_i$ mean the 3-dimensional geometry code I eventually want the model to assign to example $i$. Let $g$ denote a geometry map, and let $\theta_i$ denote a hypothetical coordinate for example $i$. If $\theta_i$ were observed, I could train with pointwise coordinate supervision:

$$
z_i \approx g(\theta_i).
$$

Instead I use a distributional target. Let $Q_1$ be the positive target geometry distribution, meaning the distribution I want positive examples to follow. Let $Q_0$ be the negative target geometry distribution, meaning the distribution I want negative examples to follow. The target is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Z_1 &= \{z_i : y_i^{country}=1\} \quad\text{should look like}\quad Q_1,\\
Z_0 &= \{z_i : y_i^{country}=0\} \quad\text{should look like}\quad Q_0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here, $Z_1$ is the set of learned geometry codes for positive target examples, and $Z_0$ is the set of learned geometry codes for negative target examples. The model is free to choose which positive example goes to which positive geometry point. What matters is that the aggregate positive distribution matches $Q_1$, and the aggregate negative distribution matches $Q_0$.

This is close in spirit to Wasserstein Auto-Encoders: the encoded aggregate distribution is matched to a chosen prior instead of assigning every example a hand-written latent coordinate [8].

### Sphere Shell

The first geometry is a sphere shell. Let $u$ be a direction sampled uniformly from the unit sphere

$$
\mathbb{S}^2=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^3:\|x\|_2=1\}
$$

The notation $\mathbb{S}^2$ means the set of points in 3-dimensional space with Euclidean norm 1. Let $\rho$ be a radius. The raw sphere point is:

$$
g_{\text{sphere}}(u,\rho)=\rho u, u\in\mathbb{S}^2\subset\mathbb{R}^3
$$

The positive class uses a smaller radius than the negative class:

$$
Q_1:\rho\approx r_1, \quad Q_0:\rho\approx r_0, \quad r_1<r_0.
$$

Here, $r_1$ is the target radius for positive examples and $r_0$ is the target radius for negative examples. $\approx$ means the sampled radius is concentrated near that value, not exactly fixed at that value. In this experiment, I use

$$
r_1=0.30,\quad r_0=0.85.
$$

**Intuition:**

- Positives live on an inner shell (blue points in Figure 6);
- Negatives live on an outer shell (reds points in Figure 6);
- The positives and negatives live in different shells so that they should not be trivially separable by one linear direction. For example, if they all live in the same sphere, the positives occupy only the north pole, and negatives occupy only the south pole, then the representation is basically linear. So I propose something symmetric like above.

### Helix Tube

The second geometry is a helix tube. Let $t\in[0,T]$ be the position along the helix with a maximum value of $T$. Let $\beta$ be the vertical pitch parameter that controls how fast the helix rises (aka how stretched). The helix core curve is:

$$
c(t)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos t\\
\sin t\\
\beta(t-T/2)
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Here, $c(t)$ is the 3-dimensional centerline of the helix.

To make a tube around the centerline, I need two directions perpendicular to the centerline. The first tube direction is:

$$
n_1(t)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos t\\
\sin t\\
0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The second tube direction is:

$$
n_2(t)=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\beta^2}}
\begin{bmatrix}
-\beta\sin t\\
\beta\cos t\\
-1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The vectors $n_1(t)$ and $n_2(t)$ are the local cross-section directions around the helix. Let $\varphi$ be the angle around that tube cross-section, with $\varphi\in[0,2\pi]$. Let $\rho$ again be the tube radius. A point in the helix tube is:

$$
g_{\text{helix}}(t,\rho,\varphi)=c(t)+\rho\left(\cos\varphi\,n_1(t)+\sin\varphi\,n_2(t)\right).
$$

The class-conditional distributions are:

$$
Q_1:t\sim \operatorname{Unif}[0,T],\quad \varphi\sim \operatorname{Unif}[0,2\pi], \quad \rho\approx r_1,
$$

$$
Q_0:t\sim \operatorname{Unif}[0,T],\quad \varphi\sim \operatorname{Unif}[0,2\pi], \quad \rho\approx r_0, \quad r_1<r_0.
$$

Here, $\operatorname{Unif}[a,b]$ means a uniform distribution on the interval from $a$ to $b$. In this experiment, I used:

$$
\beta=0.12,\quad r_1=0.15,\quad r_0=1.00
$$

**Intuition:**

- Positives live near the inner helical tube (blue points in Figure 7);
- Negatives live on a wider outer tube (red points in Figure 7);
- Both classes share the same curved scaffold;
- The label is decoded by distance to a curved structure, not by one plane.

The final helix is a one-turn helix. I first tried harder helix settings (three-turn), but they were not reliably readable by the tail layer under the other constraints. The one-turn helix is still a helix tube, but it is the simplest version that could be learned, used, and validated.

### Normalization

Either sphere and helix have different natural scales. If I do not normalize them, the model might learn from magnitude artifacts instead of geometry. Let $g(\eta)$ be a raw geometry point. The symbol $\eta$ means the parameters used to sample the point.

- For the sphere, $\eta=(u,\rho)$.
- For the helix, $\eta=(t,\rho,\varphi)$.

Since the data is balanced. I define a class balance geometry scale:

$$
\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\|g(\eta)\|_2^2\right]}.
$$

Then each geometry code $z$ is normalized so that sphere vs helix is a shape decision, not hidden norm decision:

$$
z=\frac{g(\eta)}{\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}}.
$$

Where:

- For the sphere shell:

$$
\sigma_{\text{sphere}}
=
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{2}(r_1^2+r_0^2)
+
\frac{\delta_\rho^2}{12}
}.
$$

- For the helix tube:

$$
\sigma_{\text{helix}}
=
\sqrt{
1+
\frac{\beta^2T^2}{12}
+
\frac{1}{2}(r_1^2+r_0^2)
+
\frac{\delta_\rho^2}{12}
}.
$$

Here:

- $\delta_\rho$ is the radius-noise width.
- The $1$ in the helix scale comes from the circular core $(\cos t,\sin t)$.
- The $\frac{\beta^2T^2}{12}$ term comes from the centered vertical coordinate.
- The $w^2/12$ fact is the standard variance formula for a continuous uniform distribution on an interval [9].
  - For the radius noise, the sampled jitter is centered in an interval of width $1$, then multiplied by $\delta_\rho$. So its variance contribution is:

$$
\frac{\delta_\rho^2}{12}.
$$

  - For the helix height, $t$ is uniform on $[0,T]$, so $t-T/2$ is uniform on $[-T/2,T/2]$, an interval of width $T$. Multiplying by $\beta$ gives the vertical variance:

$$
\frac{\beta^2T^2}{12}.
$$

## 3.2 Base Classifier

In this stage, I set up a base 5 layer MLP with ReLUs classifier. For text input $x_i$, it is encoded by sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 before mean-pooling into $e_i\in \mathbb{R}^{384}$. Then I feed them into a 5-layer ReLU MLP to get 8 logit outputs. This model is trained using BCE loss

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{f=1}^{8}\left[y_i^f\log\hat y_i^f+(1-y_i^f)\log(1-\hat y_i^f)\right].
$$

Here is its performance on the eight labels:

- Mean AUC: 0.9884
- Mean accuracy: 0.9616
- Country AUC: 0.9994

The target of this stage is to have an ordinary classifier that works, so later failures should come from manifold constraints rather than architecture or data handling.

## 3.3 Add the Geometry Bottleneck and Activation-Sign

### Geometry Bottleneck

In this stage, I ask whether the architecture (in Section 3.2) above can still solve the task when part of hidden2 is reserved for the geometry defined in Section 3.1.

The layer where I introduce the geometry is the same hidden layer 2 post ReLU, which is denoted as $h_i^2$. I reserve 3 hidden2 preactivation $a_i=(a_{i1},a_{i2},a_{i3})$ for example $i$. The remaining 61 coordinates are the complement $r_i$, which is not supposed to carry the target geometry.

Why 3 channels? Both target geometries live in ordinary 3D space. Using 3 channels lets hidden2 store an actual point $(x,y,z)$ on the sphere shell or helix tube. More channels would make the construction less minimal; fewer channels would not contain the requested 3D geometry.

Let $\mathcal{G}$ be the chosen geometry family, either the sphere shell or the helix tube. Let $a_\theta(e_i)$ be a learned coordinate head applied to the sentence embedding $e_i$. $\theta$ denotes the trainable parameters of the main model. Let $G_{\mathcal{G}}$ be the fixed geometry bottleneck map for geometry family $\mathcal{G}$. The geometry code is:

$$
z_i=G_{\mathcal{G}}(a_\theta(e_i)).
$$

where:

- For the sphere bottleneck, its code is

$$
z_i=\frac{\rho_i u_i}{\sigma_{\text{sphere}}},
\quad
u_i=
\begin{bmatrix}
\sqrt{1-v_i^2}\cos\theta_i\\
\sqrt{1-v_i^2}\sin\theta_i\\
v_i
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

  where:
  - $\theta_i=a_{i1}$ is azimuth angle.
  - $v_i=\tanh(a_{i2})$ is the vertical coordinate on the unit sphere.
  - $\rho_i=10^{-3}+R_{\max}\operatorname{sigmoid}(a_{i3})$, with $R_{\max}$ a maximum allowed radius.
- For the helix bottleneck, its code is

$$
z_i=\frac{g_{\text{helix}}(t_i,\rho_i,\varphi_i)}{\sigma_{\text{helix}}}
$$

  where:

$$
t_i=T\operatorname{sigmoid}(a_{i1})
$$

$$
\varphi_i=2\pi\operatorname{sigmoid}(a_{i2})
$$

$$
\rho_i=10^{-3}+R_{\max}\operatorname{sigmoid}(a_{i3})
$$

### Activation-Sign

As the hidden state is post-ReLU, so its coordinates are nonnegative. A centered sphere or helix has signed coordinates. I handle that by introducing $b_{\mathcal{G}}$, a positive offset that shifts signed coordinates into post-ReLU space. The shifted hidden2 representation is:

$$
h_i^2=[z_i+b_{\mathcal{G}},r_i].
$$

### Experiment

> Figure 9. Architecture overview when introducing geometry bottleneck.

In this stage, I still trains only the same task loss:

$$
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}.
$$

The purpose is not yet to force good class geometry. The purpose is only to ask:

*Can the classifier survive this architectural constraint?*

Then, how could we know if the model reserved its hidden state for the predefined geometry while still solving the task?

To answer this, I define these four more analyses alongside the mean AUC and country AUC above.

1. **Geometry probe.** it asks *if I only look at the 3D geometry point by ignoring the classifier and the complement channels, can I tell whether the example is country by checking whether it sits near the positive part or negative part of the target geometry?* It is a fixed geometry-based readout from only the first 3 hidden2 channels, $z$. It
   - takes the learned 3D geometry code $z$
   - converts it back to raw geometry scale
   - measures its geometry radius:
     - For sphere shell: distance from origin
     - For helix tube: distance to the nearest point on the helix core
   - asks: *is this radius closer to the positive radius or negative radius?*

$$
\text{score} = \alpha (\text{distance\_to\_neg\_radius}^2-\text{distance\_to\_pos\_radius}^2)
$$

   - computes ROC AUC between this score and country labels.

Therefore, this metric does not prove the model uses the geometry. It only proves the reserved channels contain the label in the intended geometric way.

2. **Coverage entropy.** it asks *Do the learned geometry points spread across the intended shape, or do they collapse into one small patch?*

   - For sphere shell, it
     - takes the learned 3D geometry points
     - computes their azimuth angle in the x-y plane
     - puts those angles into 8 bins around the circle
     - computes normalized entropy of the bin counts
   → High coverage entropy means the points wrap around the shell instead of all sitting on one side.

   - For helix tube, it
     - finds the nearest point on the helix core for each learned geometry point
     - converts that nearest point into a helix position $t$
     - bins $t$ into 24 bins across the full helix
     - computes normalized entropy
   → High coverage entropy means the learned points spread along the helix instead of using one small segment.

3. **Causal target delta.** it asks *if I edit only the 3 geometry channels, does the model’s own country logit move?* by

   - Replacing channels with positive-geometry anchors
   - Replacing channels with negative-geometry anchors
   - Running the tail on both edited hidden states
   - Computing their mean differences
   → A large positive value means the model’s tail is actually listening to the geometry channels for country, while a small near-zero value means the geometry may look correct, but the tail is ignoring it.

4. **Linear probe AUC.** I train a linear probe on the country’s activations and get its AUC.

### Results

As shown in Table 6, the model survives the bottleneck. However, causal delta scores for both geometries indicates that the model does not use the intended geometry. Instead, the model appears to route the label through other complement channels, suggesting that bottleneck alone is not enough.

On the other hand, low geometry probe AUC and low coverage entropy indicate that the geometry of country does not follow what we defined.

## 3.4 Add Unconditional Optimal Transport Coverage

This stage asks:

*Can the learned geometry codes cover the target geometry instead of collapsing to a small region?*

In the previous stage, the bottleneck can force every learned code to lie on the allowed geometry family, but this alone does not force the batch to use the whole geometry. For example, in the helix setting, every example can map to a valid point on the helix tube, while almost all examples still sit on the same helix segment. Therefore, the representation would technically be on the helix, but it would not be a meaningful representation of the feature.

To prevent this collapse, I add an unconditional mixture optimal transport (MixOT) loss. At this stage, I do not yet require positive examples to occupy $Q_1$, and negative examples to occupy $Q_0$. I only require the whole minibatch to spread across the overall target geometry by using

$$
Q_{\mathrm{mix}}=\pi Q_1+(1-\pi)Q_0,
$$

as an unlabeled mixture target distribution where $\pi$ is the positive rate of country in the training set.

For a minibatch $B$, let $Z_B=\{z_i:i\in B\}$ be the learned 3D geometry codes of $n$ learned points inside this batch. For each minibatch, I sample the same number of target anchors (see Section 3.1) from the mixture distribution $Q_{\mathrm{mix}}$:

$$
A_{\text{mix}}=\{\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\},\quad \alpha_j\sim Q_{\text{mix}}
$$

These anchors serve as a fresh minibatch target point cloud for the OT loss. For the sphere shell, these anchors are sampled from both the inner and outer shells. While for the helix tube, these are sampled from both tubes.

The intuition is that the learned codes are students, and the target anchors are seats laid out across the desired geometry. OT assigns students to seats while making every seat matter. If all the learned codes collapse into one corner (like in the previous stage), many anchors remain far away and the matching becomes expensive. But if the learned codes spread across the target geometry, the matching becomes cheaper.

Therefore, for a learned code $z_i$ and a target anchor $\alpha_j$, the transport cost is

$$
C_{ij}=\|z_i-\alpha_j\|_2^2
$$

Entropic OT solves

$$
\operatorname{OT}_{\varepsilon}(Z_B,A_{\text{mix}})=\min_{\Pi}\sum_{i,j}\Pi_{ij}C_{ij}+\varepsilon\sum_{i,j}\Pi_{ij}(\log\Pi_{ij}-1)
$$

subject to the balanced constraints

$$
\Pi\mathbf{1}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}, \quad \Pi^\top\mathbf{1}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}.
$$

Here $\Pi$ is the transport plan. It specifies how much mass each learned point sends to each target anchor. The first constraint ensures that every learned point is assigned. The second constraint ensures that every target anchor receives mass. This second constraint is the anti-collapse mechanism: the model cannot ignore most anchors and only match the easy ones.

The $\varepsilon$ is the entropy weight. It smooths the matching so it can be computed efficiently with Sinkhorn iterations [10].

This stage objective is therefore:

$$
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}+\lambda_{\text{mix}}\operatorname{OT}_{\varepsilon}(Z_B,A_{\text{mix}}).
$$

#### Results

In this section, I add Radius MAE to measure the mean absolute error between the learned geometry radius and the desired class radius (lower is better).

Coverage entropy is almost perfect for both geometries, so the OT seats do their anti-collapse job. But the geometry probe AUC is weak, radius MAE is still large, and causal target delta is nearly zero. This is because the unconditional OT prevents collapse and encourages the learned codes to spread out making coverage entropy to improve. Geometry probe AUC and radius MAE do not need to be good yet, as I does not yet tell positive to use $Q_1$ or negative to use $Q_0$.

## 3.5 Add Class-Conditional Geometry

This stage is to solve previous stage’s limitation:

*Put positive examples in the positive part of the shape, and put the negative examples in the negative part.*

I replace unconditional mixture matching with class-conditional matching. I also add two local pressures: a radius loss and a geometry-score loss. The intuition is that, instead of using one shared seating chart for everyone, I now give positives and negatives their own seats from $Q_1$ and from $Q_2$, respectively.

#### Class-Conditional OT (ClassOT)

To facilitate this, I reuse and modify the notations a little bit. For label $y\in\{0,1\}$, we now have subset of learned codes in the minibatch $B$ for each label as $Z_y=\{z_i:y_i=y\}$. $A_y$ be anchors sampled from $Q_y$. The class-conditional OT is:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{class-OT}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}: |Z_y|\ge 2}\operatorname{OT}_{\varepsilon}(Z_y,A_y).
$$

If fewer than two examples of a class appear in the batch, that class term is skipped for that batch.

#### Radius Loss

Class-conditional OT gives the distributional shape. However, it can still be weak as a per-example training signal. The remaining fixed MLP tail after hidden2 has to learn to decode country from the geometry code. If the geometry is only correct as a batch-level matching pattern, the tail may still not receive a simple local signal from each individual example.

So I add a radius loss. This loss says that each example should have the right class radius, not merely belong to a batch whose points can be matched to the right anchors.

Let $\operatorname{rad}_{\mathcal{G}}(z)$ be the geometry-specific radius:

- For the sphere shell, distance from the origin.
- For the helix tube, distance from the nearest helix-core point.

The radius loss is:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{radius}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i\left(\operatorname{rad}_{\mathcal{G}}(z_i)-r_{y_i}\right)^2.
$$

Here $r_{y_i}=r_1$ when $y_i=1$, and $r_{y_i}=r_0$ when $y_i=0$. In plain language, positive examples should sit near the positive radius, and negative examples should sit near the negative radius.

#### Geometry Score Loss

I also added a new geometry-score loss. This turns the geometry into a single target-facing score: is this point closer to the positive radius or to the negative radius?

Define

$$
d_1(z)=  \left(  \operatorname{rad}_{\mathcal{G}}(z)-r_1  \right)^2,  \quad d_0(z)=  \left(  \operatorname{rad}_{\mathcal{G}}(z)-r_0  \right)^2.
$$

The geometry score is

$$
s_{\mathrm{geo}}(z)=k(d_0(z)-d_1(z)).
$$

This score is high when $z$ is closer to the positive radius than the negative radius, and low when $z$ is closer to the negative radius. $k$ acts as a scale factor, making the score easier to use as a logit-like signal:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{geo-score}}=\frac{1}{N}  \sum_i  \operatorname{BCEWithLogits}  \left(  s_{\mathrm{geo}}(z_i), y_i  \right)
$$

### Geometry Score Loss Vs Radius Loss

Radius loss aims to place each example at the correct target radius. It is a geometry-shaping loss that cares about exact placement.

Geometry score loss, on the other hand, aims for the geometry itself to classify the example correctly. It turns the radius into a logit-like score by comparing distance to positive/negative radius, caring about whether the decision direction of the point is more positive-like or negative-like.

For example, suppose $r_1=0.3$ and $r_0=0.8$:

- A positive example with radius of 0.35:
  - Radius loss says: very good, close to 0.3.
  - Geometry score loss says: very good, much closer to 0.3 than 0.8.
- Another positive example with radius of 0.55:
  - Radius loss says: not ideal, move closer to 0.3.
  - Geometry score loss says: acceptable, because 0.5 is closer to 0.3 than to 0.8.

So radius loss is stricter as it controls the actual geometry. Geometry is more classifier-facing. It makes the radius rule produce the right target logit, even before the model tail learns to use it.

#### Experiment

This stage objective is therefore:

$$
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}+\lambda_{\text{class}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{class-OT}}+\lambda_{\text{radius}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{radius}}+\lambda_{\text{geo-score}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{geo-score}}
$$

I removed the previous stage’s mixture OT term to test whether class-conditional geometry can work.

#### Results

The key signal for this stage is comparison between geometry probe AUC and causal target delta. If the geometry probe AUC is high but the causal delta is tiny, then the geometry contains the labels, but the classifier is not using that geometry.

From table 8, the coverage problem is mostly solved: both geometries have high coverage entropy. The class-structure problem is also mostly solved: geometry probe AUC is almost perfect, and radius MAE is much lower than previous MixOT stage. This means the first 3 channels contain the country label in the intended geometric form.

However, the causal target is still near zero. The model can store country in the predefined geometry while the final classifier still ignores that geometry and solves it through different hidden2 signals. The linear probe AUC and country AUC support this interpretation: country remains extremely easy to recover as a linear direction from hidden2 and the model still can solve the task.

Therefore, this stage succeeds as a representation-shaping stage, but fails as a causal-use stage.

## 3.6 Make the Geometry Functional and Reduce Linear Access

**Can country remain useful while becoming harder to read as one ordinary linear direction?**

To address previous stage’s failure, I keep the task loss, and the ClassOT, bring back MixOT, and add three new pressures:

1. Causal geometry pressure,
2. Tail fitting,
3. Anti-linear pressure.

Each of these new terms targets a specific failure:

1. High geometry probe AUC but tiny causal target delta means the tail may ignore the geometry codes and solve country from other hidden2 channels.
2. High geometry probe AUC is enough if the learned geometry collapses to a small part of the target shape.
3. Linear probe AUC near 1 means country is still easy to recover as plain hidden2 direction.

This is also why I bring MixOT coverage back at this stage. In principle, ClassOT should already carry the geometry: positives match $Q_1$ and negatives match $Q_0$. In practice, this stage is no longer a clean geometry-only experiment as causal pressures, adversaries, leakage penalties, and tail fitting are added. These forces can pull the representation toward an easier causal solution, even if that solution only uses a small convenient region of the manifold, especially for the helix.

### Causal Geometry Pressure

The first new pressure asks a direct intervention question during training:

**If I replace only the geometry channels, does the model’s own country logit move?**

I keep the non-geometry complement $r_i$ fixed and replace only the first 3 geometry channels. This creates two edited hidden states: one with a positive geometry anchor and one with a negative geometry anchor.

$$
h_i^+=[\alpha_i^1+b_{\mathcal{G}},r_i],  \qquad  \alpha_i^1\sim Q_1,
$$

and

$$
h_i^-=[\alpha_i^0+b_{\mathcal{G}},r_i],  \qquad  \alpha_i^0\sim Q_0.
$$

Let $C(\cdot)$ be the model’s tail from hidden2 to logits. The target pressure asks the tail to respond correctly as $C_{f^\star}(h_i^+)$ should be high while $\qquad  C_{f^\star}(h_i^-)$ should be low. As that, we have target causal response loss

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{target-causal}}=\operatorname{BCEWithLogits}(C_{f^\star}(h^+),1)+\operatorname{BCEWithLogits}(C_{f^\star}(h^-),0)
$$

Intuitively, if I swap in a positive geometry code, the country logit should rise, and vice versa [11,12].

However, changing the geometry should mainly affect country, not every output. So I also add an off-target spillover penalty:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{spill-causal}}=\frac{1}{7N}\sum_i\sum_{f\ne f^\star}\left(C_f(h_i^+)-C_f(h_i^-)\right)^2
$$

Both pressure the model’s tail to response when $z_i$ is changed

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{causal}}=\mathcal L_{\text{target-edit}}+0.5\mathcal L_{\text{spill}}
$$

### Tail Fitting

Tail fitting is narrower. It freezes the earlier layers, and trains only the existing final tail

*hidden2 → hidden 3 → logits*

Given the controlled hidden2 states, it pushes the tail to learn how to decode the geometry channels into the country logit. It is not mainly about shaping the geometry. It assumes the geometry code is already there, then teaches the final layer how to read it.

Causal geometry pressure and tail fitting are not redundant because the model can fail in two separate ways:

1. **Good geometry, bad use.** The 3 geometry channels contain the right geometry structure, but the tail ignores them. Causal geometry pressure alone is too indirect, the model could
   - still lean on existing non-gemetry features, or
   - the tail might not learn a clean decoder from the 3 channels.
  → Tail fitting gives a focused direction for the decoder to immediately use the geometry channels.
2. **Bad geometry, good reader.** The tail is willing to read the geometry channels, but the channels themselves do not form the desired geometry structure.
  → Tail fitting cannot fix this. Geometry losses and causal geometry pressure help here.

A simple analogy is that:

- **Geometry losses** $\mathcal L_{\text{mix-OT}}, \mathcal L_{\text{radius}}, \mathcal L_{\text{causal}},$: teach the notes to be written in the right language.
- **Causal geometry pressure** $\mathcal L_{\text{causal}}$: checks that changing the note changes the model’s answer.
- **Tail fitting:** teaches the final reader how to read that language.

Tail fitting uses two controlled version of $h_i^2=[z_i+b_{\mathcal G},r_i]$ hidden states:

- **Context state:** $h_i^{\text{ctx}}=[z_i+b_{\mathcal G},\operatorname{stopgrad}(r_i)]$. This lets the tail fit on complement values for each example, but prevents learning signal from flowing back to earlier layers to hide country information in these complement channels.
- **Neutral state:** $h_i^{\text{neu}}=[z_i+b_{\mathcal G},\operatorname{stopgrad}(\bar r)]$, where $\bar r$ is the minibatch mean complement vector. If the model tail can still predict country from this state, the example-specific signal must come from the geometry channels $z_i$, because the complement no longer carries example-specific clues.

Then the tail fitting loss has two parts:

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{tail}}=\mathcal L^{\text{label}}_{\text{tail}} + \mathcal L^{\text{score}}_{\text{tail}}
$$

The model tail must predict the target label $y_i\in\{0,1\}$ from both controlled states. As $C_{f^*}(\cdot)$ is the country logit produced by the model tail, then:

$$
\mathcal L^{\text{label}}_{\text{tail}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\operatorname{BCEWithLogits}(C_{f^\star}(h_i^{\text{ctx}}),y_i)+\operatorname{BCEWithLogits}(C_{f^\star}(h_i^{\text{neu}}),y_i)\right]
$$

The target logit is trained to follow the intended geometry score. As $s_{\mathcal G}(z_i)$ should be high when $z_i$ is near the positive target radius and low when $z_i$ is near the negative target radius. Tail fitting adds:

$$
\mathcal L^{\text{score}}_{\text{tail}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\operatorname{SmoothL1}(C_{f^\star}(h^{\text{ctx}}),\operatorname{stopgrad}(s_{\mathcal G}(z)))+\operatorname{SmoothL1}(C_{f^\star}(h^{\text{neu}}),\operatorname{stopgrad}(s_{\mathcal G}(z)))\right]
$$

The $\operatorname{stopgrad}$ on $s_{\mathcal G}(z_i)$ means this score is treated as a fixed teacher signal. The model tail must move toward the score; the geometry score itself is not adjusted to make the loss easier.

### Anti-linear Pressure

The second major failure of the previous stage is the linear shortcut. Even after the geometry contains country, the full hidden2 vector may still contain a simple direction that predicts country. If that direction remains too strong, the classifier can keep using it instead of the intended geometry pathway.

This stage adds a linear adversary on the full hidden2 state. The adversary is a one-layer classifier that tries to predict country from hidden2. Let

$$
a_\phi(h_i^2)=\sigma(w^\top h_i^2+b)
$$

be the adversary’s predicted probability. The adversary loss is

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{lin-adv}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\operatorname{BCEWithLogits}(a_\phi(\operatorname{GRL}(h_i^2)),y_i)
$$

Equivalently, in probability form,

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{lin-adv}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\left[y_i\log\sigma(a_\phi(h_i^2))+(1-y_i)\log(1-\sigma(a_\phi(h_i^2)))\right]
$$

The important part is $\operatorname{GRL}$, the gradient-reversal layer. In the forward pass, $\operatorname{GRL}(h_i^2)=h_i^2$, so the adversary sees the real hidden2 vector. In the backward pass, the gradient sent into $h_i^2$ is multiplied by $-1$. Therefore the adversary parameters $\phi$ are trained to minimize the prediction loss, but the representation parameters are trained in the opposite direction: they are pushed to make this linear readout worse. Conceptually, this is the gradient-reversal version of the following min-max objective:

$$
\min_{\theta}  \max_{\phi}  \quad  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}}  +  \lambda_{\mathrm{geom}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{geom}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{lin}}  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{lin\text{-}adv}}(\phi;\theta)
$$
The intuition is simple: the adversary keeps asking, “Can I recover country from a straight line through hidden2?" If yes, gradient reversal pushes hidden2 to erase that straight-line shortcut. But the main task loss still requires the model to predict country, and the geometry losses still require the first three hidden2 channels to carry the target geometry. So the desired resolution is not to delete country information entirely; it is to make the classifier rely less on an arbitrary linear shortcut and more on the predefined geometry pathway.

This follows the gradient-reversal mechanism introduced in domain-adversarial neural networks by Ganin et al. [13], where a representation is trained to be useful for the main task while being uninformative to an adversarial classifier for an unwanted attribute/source signal.

### Squared Correlation Penalty

I also include squared correlation penalties as a simpler check for country leakage. Even if the adversary fails to detect leakage, this penalty discourages any single hidden2 channel from becoming strongly correlated with country.

The squared correlation penalties are important because adversarial training can miss simple leakage. A linear adversary is trained by optimization, so it can underfit, get stuck, or fail to notice one narrow channel that quietly tracks country. Correlation is a more direct diagnostic and penalty: for each hidden channel, check whether its value rises or falls with the target label, then penalize that squared association. This does not prove the complement is clean, but it closes an easy shortcut where the model hides country in one coordinate while the geometry channels only appear to be responsible.

For hidden2 channel j, define:

$$
\operatorname{Corr}(h_{\cdot j}^2,y)=\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i (h_{ij}^2-\bar h_j^2)(y_i-\bar y)}{\operatorname{std}(h_{\cdot j}^2)\operatorname{std}(y)}
$$

The hidden2 leak penalty is:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{leak}}=\sum_{j=1}^{64}\operatorname{Corr}(h^2_{\cdot j},y)^2
$$

where $h^2_{\cdot k}$ is hidden2 channel $k$ across the batch.

#### Experiment

This stage objective is therefore:

$$
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}+\lambda_{\text{class}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{class-OT}}+\lambda_{\text{radius}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{radius}}+\lambda_{\text{geo-score}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{geo-score}}+\lambda_{\text{tail}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{tail}}+\lambda_{\text{lin-adv}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin-adv}}+\lambda_{\text{leak}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{leak}}
$$

#### Results

Country AUC and mean task AUC stay high, so the model does not sacrifice the task. Geometry probe AUC stays high, so the target label is still present in the intended geometry. Causal target delta jumps from near zero in the previous stage to 3.0845 for the sphere and 1.8980 for the helix, so geometry edits now move the model’s own country logit.

The linear shortcut is also much weaker. In the previous stage of ClassOT, linear probe AUC is also 1 for both geometries. In this stage, it drops hard to 0.5743 for the sphere and 0.6710 for the helix. This does not mean the representation contains no linear information. bit it is no longer the trivial readout from the previous stage.

The helix result is less clean than the sphere result. Its coverage entropy is 0.7510 and its linear probe is higher. This is why I reintroduce MixOT coverage back on as the causal use is not enough if the learned helix no longer covers the predefined helix.

## 3.7 Reduce Complement Shortcuts

After GFAL, linear access from the full hidden2 vector is weaker and geometry interventions work, but one shortcut can still remain: the complement channels.

**If I remove the geometry code $z_i$ and look only at the remaining 61 $r_i$ channels, can I still read country?**

If the answer is yes, then the geometry may be functional but not primary. The model can use the geometry pathway, but it also has a backup route through the complement. Stage 5 adds a second adversary to pressure this shortcut directly. Let $a_\psi(r_i)$ be a one-layer complement adversary logit, where $\psi$ are the adversary parameters. Its binary prediction is $\sigma(c_\psi(r_i))$, and its loss is

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{comp-adv}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\operatorname{BCEWithLogits}(c_\psi(\operatorname{GRL}(r_i)),y_i^{f^\star})
$$

Equivalently, in probability form,

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{comp-adv}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\left[y_i^{f^\star}\log\sigma(c_\psi(r_i))+(1-y_i^{f^\star})\log(1-\sigma(c_\psi(r_i)))\right]
$$

This also goes with complement correlation penalty

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{comp-leak}}=\sum_{k=1}^{61}\operatorname{corr}(r_{\cdot k},y)^2
$$

### Why Does Anti-linear Adversary and Complement Adversary Coexist?

Why don't I remove the anti-linear adversary and use only complement adversary? Because complement cleanup is narrower. The complement adversary only looks at $r_i$ non-geometry channels.

The complement adversary asks ‘can country be read after removing the geometry channels?’

The anti-linear adversary asks ‘is country still easy to read as a simple linear direction from the whole hidden2 state?’

The first question is important but cannot replace the broader question from the anti-linear adversary. The whole hidden2 state contains both $z_i+b_{\mathcal G}$ and $r_i$, and the tail classifier sees both. If I remove the full hidden anti-linear pressure, the model can reopen an easy linear shortcut using a mixture of geometry $z_i+b_{\mathcal G}$ and complement channels $r_i$.

The squared correlation penalty is kept for the same reason. The adversary attacks a learned linear readout, but it depends on optimization. The correlation penalty attacks a simpler failure directly: one hidden2 channel quietly tracks country. While in stage, I am adding complement-specific pressure, not declaring the earlier shortcut solved forever. Keeping both is a guardrail: while the model learns to clean up $r_i$, it is still not allowed to make country trivially exposed again in the full representation.

#### Experiment

This stage objective is therefore:

$$
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}+\lambda_{\text{class}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{class-OT}}+\lambda_{\text{radius}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{radius}}+\lambda_{\text{geo-score}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{geo-score}}+\lambda_{\text{tail}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{tail}}+\lambda_{\text{lin-adv}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin-adv}}+\lambda_{\text{leak}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{leak}}+\lambda_{\text{comp-adv}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{comp-adv}}+\lambda_{\text{comp-leak}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{comp-leak}}
$$

#### Results

The task still works as both the country AUC and the mean task AUC remain high. The geometry is still present as the geometry probe AUC stays near 1 for both. The geometry is also still functional: causal target delta increase for both gemetries.

The main improvement is the complement probe. For the helix, complement AUC drops from 0.6030 to 0.5661. For the sphere, it rises from 0.5764 to 0.6086. So this stage strengthens the casual geometry signal for both geometries and reduces complement access for the helix but does not eliminate it completely for both. This means that the final model does not have perfect information isolation. It proves that the intended geometry is a strong and causally active pathway, while the non-geometry complement is too weak to be the simple explanation of the result.

## 3.8 Causal-Use Validation

*If I edit only the geometry channels after training, does the trained classifier actually follow that edit?*

Good probes from the previous stage are still not enough. I need direct evidences that changing geometry causes the model’s country output to move. Therefore, I freeze the model and perform hidden2 interventions that edit only the geometry channels.

First, I select a balanced held-out subset then run five checks:

1. Ablate the geometry code by replacing $z_i$ with zero;
2. Replace $z_i$ with a positive anchor $\alpha^1 \sim Q_1$, or with a negative anchor $\alpha^0 \sim Q_0$;
3. Swap geometry codes between positive and negative examples;
4. Move along a positive-to-negative path through the geometry;
5. Measure off-target movement in the other 7 logits;

Each check asks a slightly different version of the same question:

1. **Ablation:** asks whether moving the geometry damages target prediction;
2. **Replacement:** asks whether positive versus negative geometry anchors move the target logit;
3. **Swap:** asks whether exchanging geometry codes between labels moves predictions in the expected directions;
4. **Path:** asks whether smoothly moving through the geometry produces a smooth target-logit movement;
5. **Specify:** asks whether the target moves much more than other 7 outputs.

### Ablation Check Through Ablation AUC Drop

Ablation target AUC is computed after replacing $z_i$ with zero. Ablation AUC drop is:

$$
\operatorname{AUC}_{\text{base}}-\operatorname{AUC}_{\text{ablated}}
$$

This answers *If I remove the geometry signal, does country prediction get worse?*

### Replacement Check Through Causal Target Delta

Replacement check is causal target delta, which is already defined in Section 3.3; I restate it here with a formal definition for completeness:

$$
h_i^2\to\tilde h_i^2=[\alpha_i^1+b_{\mathcal{G}},r_i], \quad\text{or}\quad  h_i^2\to\tilde h_i^2=[\alpha_i^0+b_{\mathcal{G}},r_i]
$$

Here $\tilde h_i^2$ is the intervened hidden2 state. The complement $r_i$ is copied from the original example, so the only intended cause of output movement is the replacement geometry code.

Then causal target delta is

$$
\Delta_{\text{target}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i\left[C_{f^\star}([\alpha_i^1+b_{\mathcal{G}},r_i])-C_{f^\star}([\alpha_i^0+b_{\mathcal{G}},r_i])\right]
$$

where $C_{f^\star}(h)$ is the country logit by the model tail from hidden2.

### Swap Check Through Positive-Negative Pairs

Swap target shift pairs positives and negatives, exchanges only their geometry channels, and measures whether positive examples drop while negative examples rise. From the held-out examples, let $P$ be the selected positive indices and $N$ be the selected negative indices, with paris $(p_j,n_j)$. The original hidden states are:

$$
h_{p_j}^2=[z_{p_j}+b_{\mathcal G},r_{p_j}],\quad h_{n_j}^2=[z_{n_j}+b_{\mathcal G},r_{n_j}]
$$

Now swap only the geometry channels:

$$
\tilde h_{p_j}^2=[z_{n_j}+b_{\mathcal G},r_{p_j}],\quad\tilde h_{n_j}^2=[z_{p_j}+b_{\mathcal G},r_{n_j}]
$$

The swap target shift is:

$$
\Delta_{\text{swap}}=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left[C_{f^\star}(h_{p_j}^2)-C_{f^\star}(\tilde h_{p_j}^2)+C_{f^\star}(\tilde h_{n_j}^2)-C_{f^\star}(h_{n_j}^2)\right]
$$

where $m$ is the number of positive-negative pairs. The first term checks that positive examples drop when given negative geometry. The second term checks that negative examples rise when given positive geometry.

### Path Monotonic Fraction By Gradually Increasing Radius

First, I choose a deterministic path through the target geometry from the positive radius to the negative radius:

$$
z^{(1)},z^{(2)},\ldots,z^{(K)}
$$

The intuition is that instead of jumping directly from a positive geometry point to a negative geometry point, I draw a small line/curve of intermediate geometry point between them and check whether the model’s country logit changes smoothly along the way. The label in both geometries is mainly carried by radius (with positives in smaller radius and negatives in larger radius). I first fix the start point and only change the radius step by step.

Sphere: pick one direction (eg. positive x-axis), then use radii between the positive radius and the negative radius $r_1 < \text{…} < r_k$. So the model sees ‘inner shell point → slightly larger shell point → … → outer shell point’.

Helix: it is similar. It picks one fixed position along the helix and one fixed tube angle. Then it only increases the tube radius.

This is done deterministically by choosing the same direction every time, then sweeps radius from the positive class radius to the negative class radius.

Then I fix the complement channels by using the mean complement $\bar r$, and form:

$$
h^{(k)}=[z^{(k)}+b_{\mathcal G},\bar r]
$$

Let $\ell_k=C_{f^\star}(h^{(k)})$ be the country logit at step $k$. Since the path moves from positive geometry to negative geometry, the expected behavior is that country logit decreases. The path target delta is

$$
\Delta_{\text{path}}=\ell_1-\ell_K
$$

The path monotonic fraction is:

$$
M_{\text{path}}=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\mathbf{1}\left[\ell_k-\ell_{k+1}>0\right]
$$

$M_{\text{path}}=1$ means every adjacent step moved in the expected direction.

#### Specificity

Specificity compares target movement with off-target movement:

$$
\operatorname{specificity}=\frac{|\Delta_{\text{target}}|}{\frac{1}{7N}\sum_i\sum_{f\ne f^\star}\left|C_f([\alpha_i^1+b_{\mathcal{G}},r_i])-C_f([\alpha_i^0+b_{\mathcal{G}},r_i])\right|}
$$

The denominator is the average movement of the other 7 logits. A high specificity ratio means the geometry edit mainly affects country, not every feature at once.

#### Results

For the sphere, removing the geometry nearly destroys country prediction: AUC falls from 0.9996 by 0.4675 to 0.5317, close to chance. Replacing negative geometry with positive geometry moves the country logit by 3.5004 on average, and the specificity ratio is 73.4252, meaning the edit affects country far more than the other 7 logits. The path monotonic fraction is 1.0000, so every step from positive radius to negative radius moves the target logit in the expected direction. Together, these numbers say the sphere code is not decorative; the classifier depends on it.

For the helix, the same causal pattern holds. Ablation drops country AUC by 0.4279, leaving ablated AUC at 0.5654. Positive-versus-negative geometry replacement moves the target logit by 2.6884, with specificity 47.3151, again showing target-specific control rather than global disruption. The path monotonic fraction is 1.0000, so the helix radius behaves as a smooth control variable. The helix is geometrically weaker than the sphere because coverage entropy is lower and radius MAE is higher, but its causal-use evidence is still strong.

The swap metrics are especially important because they use the model’s own learned geometry codes, not only synthetic anchors.

For the sphere, swapping positive and negative geometry codes moves the country logit by 4.4433 on average, while the swap specificity ratio is 92.0223;

For the helix, the target shift is even larger at 7.1863, with specificity 128.0430.

These large specificity ratios mean the swap changes country far more than the other 7 logits. So the learned geometry channel is not just visually aligned with the label; exchanging it between examples produces the expected causal movement in the model’s own prediction path.
